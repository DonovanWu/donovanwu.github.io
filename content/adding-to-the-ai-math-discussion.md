## LLM's Limitations and Strengths

Before any discussion begins, I feel it's important to address the limitations of LLMs. People seem to have stopped questioning if LLMs will be capable of doing certain things, and understandably so. Like, surely whatever I'm going to say will no longer be true in a few months, right? Then I'd like to point you to [a blog post I wrote two years ago](/blog.html?p=chat-gpts-mental-breakdown) explaining why ChatGPT seemed to have occasional "mental breakdowns" at the time, in which I listed two major LLM limitations. Both of them still stand today:
* **LLMs lack physical world input**: Even multi-modal models ingest at most video and audio data during their training, but humans have a lot more sensories to help us build our understanding of the real world and human experiences, such as sense of smell, taste, and touch.
* **Training AI is still solving a mathematical optimization problem**: Absolute optimization usually isn't how nature (including humans) handles things, because it requires perfect information and usually that's not what you can get, and yet training AI is still essentially doing some mathematical optimization - because that's the easy thing to do.

If you have understood where LLMs might hit a wall, it is obvious what they _should_ be good at: It is the abstract stuff. If LLMs would frequently get impeded by a requirement of the understanding of the physical world, then it's harder for them to replace humans. But unfortunately, mathematics is all abstract, and doesn't really need to be supported by a physical world. In fact, I believe math is all made-up, that it is essentially just an insanely robust system to create rules that end up being [unreasonably effective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Unreasonable_Effectiveness_of_Mathematics_in_the_Natural_Sciences). But that's inherently a philosophical discussion, so no more comments here. Maybe we'll come back to it later.

The bottom line is, LLM is no magic. Though with each achievement attained previously thought to be impossible, it's harder and harder to ask people to not treat LLMs like magic. What we have been flabbergasted by for the past few years are all improvements made by scaling up the model, and some clever engineering around that, rather than <mark title="By which I mean models will probably need to evolve from the Transformer architecture. Something like JEPA may be a start.">fundamental changes to the models themselves</mark>. Clever engineering can get you much further than you'd think, actually, not too unlike how, in a bubble, the market can stay unreasonable for longer than you'd expect.

## It Really Is All About the Journey

"It's about the journey, not the destination." A saying as old as time. As corny as this might sound, I think this point need to be delivered home harder in many conversations regarding how AI will impact the field of mathematics. Though it is understandable why people don't quote it more often. <mark title='And by the way, this is probably how the "mathematicians did not ask for this work to be done" response happened... And why that is, is because the population at large has certain cynical assumptions about the society we live in, that those in power, be it your boss, your manager, or those who give funding to academia, eventually only demand outputs. And output is the destination you reach, not the journey. I'm not trying to refute this here. Actually, let's just say that this cynical assumption is true. Nevertheless, one should always check and re-examine their hidden assumptions. And the hidden assumption being made is that output from the mathematical field is simply proving theorems. Well, I'd argue it is **NOT**.

And I don't even have to argue this myself. 3Blue1Brown (a.k.a. Grant Sanderson) has already made a video detailing his view on humans' role in mathematics in the AI era, in which this is one of the points he made. I really recommend to watch this video in full:

A thing from this podcast I would like to mention is an interesting thought experiment brought up around 2:35:38 by Steven Brunton (Go Dawgs!!!), when Lex Fridman explicitly asked whether math is invented or discovered. Brunton asked if numbers are real. And if your answer is, of course, I can count one rock, two rocks, three rocks... Then think again, because rocks are collections of atoms, and different rocks may also have wildly different compositions, resulting in different shapes and colors, but you have no problem counting them as one rock, two rocks, and so on. So pretty quickly, it becomes clear that number is a type of abstraction, and therefore... a human invention, perhaps?

If that messes with your brain, here's a thought experiment I would personally like to present. Do circles exist? Pretty soon, one will realize that, in the real world, nothing truly matches the mathematical description of a circle. Like, the base of your mug might look like a smooth circle, but if you zoom in, you are guaranteed to find some imperfections on the edges, some microscopic jaggedness. Even if its craftsmanship is perfect, matters themselves are not infinitely divisible. So... it's pretty clear that a perfect circle exists only in mathematical description. And by that extension, is <span class="formula">π</span> even real (not as in if it's a real number, but as in "real" real)? One could say, even if it's imagined, it is useful nonetheless, like, when calculating things like perimeters and areas, <span class="formula">π</span> can help us come up with a theoretical value that's pretty close to the real value. But in that case, does <span class="formula">π</span> really need to be an irrational number, having an infinite number of digits with no pattern after the decimal point? Let's say, trying to fathom infinite digits after the decimal point just hurts my brain, and in real-world engineering, 100 digits after the decimal point is far beyond any of our precision need anyway, so let's make every number end after 100 digits after the decimal point, including <span class="formula">π</span>. So there's no irrational numbers, in fact, not even rational numbers with repeating decimals. But pretty soon you run into problems. Say, "10 over 3 times 3" and "10 times 3 over 3" aren't going to have the same answer. So there you have it. Even though in practice, 100 digits after the decimal point is enough for precision needs, you do have to have, say, irrational numbers and other stuff to keep mathematics consistent. So does <span class="formula">π</span> exist in the physical world? Maybe. But I can totally see a world that can still go around while <span class="formula">π</span> is a completely made-up concept.

So what does this have to do with AI? Well, if we accept that math is invented, it has some consequences. One, previously mentioned LLM limitations will totally not apply here. Math becomes a bunch of symbols and nothing more, not even really needing a physical world to support it. And thus, LLMs likely can simply get better at math just by digesting more math literature. Perhaps it can even achieve the so-called recursive self-improvements (RSI) very easily in the field of mathematics specifically. Two, inventing (new) math also becomes a possibility. I think it's quite likely to happen, especially if the "RSI in math" scenario comes true. It will probably not be long before LLMs start adding new axioms to ZFC in order to solve certain problems. However, this is where we should probably make sure math remains to be created by humans for humans. If humans cannot understand the new axiom being added, then we probably shouldn't add it. In fact, if such a thing indeed come true, I predict that LLMs from different companies and labs will probably propose different axioms to add to ZFC, which will likely turn out to be mutually incompatible (but probably not obvious at first), because it is very easy and tempting to invent a bad system to solve an immediate problem. Or at least that's what my experience in software engineering tells me. In my experience, AI can also absolutely amplify bad habits in software development ten-fold. A lot of times, even some very tech savvy people can be fooled by AI. So, it won't surprise me if it does the same thing to the mathematical community. Fortunately, mathematicians are showing a lot of caution towards AI right now, and I think that's only a good thing.

## The Real Revolution That Isn't Getting Enough Attention

For most people, what they got out of the recent OpenAI math news is perhaps nothing but the sheer awe that AI seemed to have solved problems they couldn't even comprehend. For those who have been keeping up with development in the field of mathematics for some time, though, there's an interesting detail that immediately sticks out: that many of the proofs provided by OpenAI came with Lean formalization. Lean is a programming language that can represent a mathematical proof, and that is to say, one can straight out write proofs using this programming language, and <mark title="Technically, it's a bit more nuanced. What the compiler essentially checks is if every step in the program is logically correct. You can still have bogus proof if the provided assumptions are wrong to begin with, or if your Lean program doesn't exactly translate to your proof written in natural language. Nevertheless, it goes without saying that this is a powerful tool to use when it comes to complicated proofs.">the program will only compile if the proof is correct</mark>. I first heard about it in the following podcast <mark title="Speak of whom, I got my degree in Computer Science at UCLA and now I regret not having been to his lecture at least once. But, you know... I got very depressed during grad school, so couldn't exactly find the energy to sneak into lectures that aren't part of schoolwork. Nevertheless... Go Bruins!">Terrence Tao</mark> did with Lex Fridman more than a year before all these AI math things came out:

(Funny how Terrence Tao often came under fire in the recent AI math debate and being accused of "gate-keeping" while not that long ago he was all for using AI in math and some very new tools. More evidence that the current debate isn't very reasonable.)

In the video, Terrence Tao-ted (sorry if the pun isn't funny LOL) how Lean could enable mathematicians to collaborate on complicated proofs. He even mentioned how Lean is like "an extremely pedantic colleague", which is something anyone with some software development experience can relate to, no matter what language is used. Anyway, the gist is this: The old way of doing mathematics heavily favors solo work, because it isn't exactly easy for mathematicians to check on their collaborator's work, as everyone involved likely have different areas of expertise, but if all parties can write their proofs in Lean, a machine can then help to check if those proofs could be put together to prove a bigger theorem. Problem is, it takes a lot of time and effort for mathematicians to adopt Lean. Understandably so, though. When I was in university, in CS classes, we often hear how math students can't get over with basic code syntax such as `x = x + 1`. While I'm not sure how true this is, or if it's popular for CS major to try to mock math major (LOL), in software engineering, we do consciously avoid a thing called "anti-pattern"... because it's bad, duh. `x = x + 1` is not a programmer's anti-pattern, but certainly _is_ an anti-pattern for math students. And then, even if Lean has carefully avoided those anti-patterns in its design, not every existing mathematician is willing to learn to code. This became where AI caught on, as machines don't have such human weaknesses. That being said, I think it goes without saying that the advance in AI theorem proving owes a debt of gratitude towards the groundwork laid by the development of Lean (and Mathlib, which made Lean much easier to use). The existence of the language enabled LLMs to do math proofs in a more agentic way and check on its hallucinations, which makes their output much more reliable.

But even without Lean, it has been a long-time trend in the field of mathematics that more and more papers are done in collaboration rather than having a solo author. This has been mentioned in the first Lex Fridman podcast shared on this page around 2:21:00. This is the real revolution that's happening, in my opinion, considering that doing things solo has been the way math studies move forward for thousands of years in human history. The ancient people couldn't do it because, first, people who could push the boundaries of math are rare, and second, they don't have this thing called... Internet. As much as we'd like to say that much of the Internet feels like a toxic cesspool, people can really achieve great things if a group of similar-minded and highly passionate people come together. That last sentence, I almost wanted to quote word by word from this [vlogbrothers video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=twS0SrDg-fc) instead, where John Green pondered why players were suddenly getting extremely good at the 1989 NES version of Tetris. For some people, it may seem strange how a video game can have connections with math, but it totally makes sense to me. Their shapes may be different, but the essences are the same. As a matter of fact, if you look at NES Tetris player's Discord, some of their discussions are highly technical, requiring a level of understanding in reverse engineering / operating systems that even bona fide software developers aren't likely going to have. For instance, try [this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BpEcjdr_YDo) produced by one of the high-level player. Just like math, in some sense, it's all nerds talking about nerdy stuff. And just like Tetris, Discord is also the place where one of the most noticeable math breakthrough happened recently. Yes, you heard that right, Discord, a platform for gamers, of all places. And the math breakthrough I'm talking about, is the proof of the <span class="formula">BB(5)</span> value, BB being the [Busy Beaver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Busy_beaver) function.

And since then, not only have we solved <span class="formula">BB(5)</span>, our understanding about <span class="formula">BB(6)</span> also improved by quite a lot:
* In 2014, the known lower bound of <span class="formula">BB(6)</span> apparently sat at somewhere around <span class="formula">10<sup>10,500</sup></span> according to this [Computerphile video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CE8UhcyJS0I)

* As of 2026, the lower bound was further pushed to <span class="formula">10↑↑10↑↑10↑↑8</span> (and I'll just put the [bbchallenge wiki](https://wiki.bbchallenge.org/wiki/BB(6)) link here and hope you're accessing it in 2026...), and multiple [cryptids](https://wiki.bbchallenge.org/wiki/Cryptids) have been identified and studied, namely, some 6-state Turing Machines are shown to be equivalent to questions that are similar to famously unsolved math problems

Note: All three values shown above (and in the linked videos) are at least how large the <span class="formula">Σ(n)</span> is for Busy Beaver function, i.e. how many 1's are printed on the tape when the machine halts, while the embedded video uses <span class="formula">S(n)</span>, which is the number of steps taken when the machine halts. While <span class="formula">S(n)</span> is always at least as large as <span class="formula">Σ(n)</span> (because the best case is the machine prints one 1 per step and no 0's), since Busy Beaver function is proven to grow faster than any computable function, both values will get larger than the output of any computable function with sufficiently large <span class="formula">n</span>, even for something as ridiculous as <span class="formula">TREE(n)</span>.

It's worth mentioning that AI is heavily used in the research of <span class="formula">BB(6)</span>. And I think bbchallenge community is an excellent example how AI could be used as an assistant to humans, rather than something that competes with humans and potentially replaces them. However, one must also understand that what the bbchallenge community is doing cannot exactly scale to the entire math community. Most of the resources they need are provided by volunteers, people who already have a job and likely stable income, are intrigued by some math problems that don't have apparent real-world applications, are willing to part their money for it and ask for no return, and also happen to possess the expertise required to help in the cause to begin with. Hopefully it should be abundantly clear to you now, that a far more stable and sustainable way to fund researches is through institutions, via grants and tenure and, you know, all the "boring" stuff. That's likely how some bbchallenge community members could contribute to the project, too, that they work in the academia to begin with, so they have the right know-how as well as a little spare money. In this light, it's not hard to see why there's so much backlash against AI math. People's academic careers are in jeopardy! So, it's never really about humans being unhappy because AI could do a better job, but if the AI companies keep doing what they are doing, and actually achieve what they promise to achieve, it is for sure to throw off the balance of the current system, which will definitely need some major restructuring of human society in order to fix it. And yet, nobody's come up with a convincing enough vision how that society will be like.

## An Era We Must Tread Carefully

Okay, I'm going to talk about what I think the real danger of AI is. So this is going to be a highly opinionated section with a significant dose of negativity. You may end here if you want to wind up on a more positive note (though I'm not sure how well I have done that).

As I said at the beginning of this blog post, I'm not exactly a "centrist" when it comes to AI issues. And what I mean by that is... generally the "pilled" side just pisses me off more. Well, I live in China, and the vibe in the media here is usually much friendlier towards AI than Western media... which is honestly driving me insane because I have half a brain cell that does critical thinking that cannot be turned off. To say these people are "pilled" is a severe understatement. Look, I'm not saying that things will stay the same. In fact, it's most certainly that things will never go back to the way they were, even if the AI bubble pops. But sci-fi isn't exactly going to come true, either. To be fair, it does feel like sci-fi becomes reality sometimes, and that's exactly the kind of thing that give hallucinations to humans that make them so "pilled" that it's hard to wake up.

Like [this guy](https://x.com/Noe1017_Monad/status/2112008540592410951) who thinks math is solved. And its elegance? Merely "a crutch" due to "human brain capacity". AI will be able to brute force everything anyway. From the mention of God at the end of the post, it is not hard to see that they, to say the very least, treated AI like magic. It for sure feels like magic sometimes, I'll admit. And that's exactly the danger, as I have just argued. You'll also notice that they seemingly used some math-y words in their arguments, too. Really reminds me of [people selling "enterprise skills"](https://news.qq.com/rain/a/20260323A07JII00) during the early days when agentic AI "skill" was first conceptualized: usually those people know all the buzzwords by heart, but can't tell you what "stochastic gradient descent" is. And the post author clearly didn't get the memo. First, there's [Gödel's incompleteness theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel%27s_incompleteness_theorems), so literally not all math problems could be solved. We just don't know which ones aren't solvable. Second, even for solvable math problems, they may involve stupendously large numbers that make brute forcing just not an option. Like <span class="formula">BB(6)</span> we just mentioned above! Even its previous lower bound <span class="formula">10↑↑15</span> is quite literally larger than any astronomical number! I'm not joking. The Poincaré recurrence time of the observable universe is on the scale of only around <span class="formula">10↑↑5</span> years. What that mean is, in this much time, the universe could end in [heat death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_death_of_the_universe) and be reborn again in a new big bang caused by random quantum fluctuation. And yet you have <span class="formula">10↑↑15</span> steps to take. You can advance one step in a Planck time, which is roughly 10<sup>-43</sup> seconds, and the universe will still need to "reboot" roughly <span class="formula">10↑↑15</span> times for you to finish (and notice how the number didn't even change). Also, don't forget that the lower bound has been raised to over <span class="formula">10↑↑10↑↑10↑↑8</span> now. Also, this is still significantly smaller than [Graham's number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham%27s_number), which is in turn much smaller than <span class="formula">TREE(3)</span>. And the TREE function is still a computable function! With <span class="formula">TREE(1) = 1</span> and <span class="formula">TREE(2) = 3</span>, its growth rate is still going to be completely dwarfed by Busy Beaver after some <span class="formula">n</span> (which might as well be <mark title="I did the meme!">6 or 7</mark>). And Goldbach Conjecture can be written as a 25-state Turing machine, which means that if we know the value of <span class="formula">BB(25)</span>, we can run it for this many steps and see if it halts, and if not, Goldbach Conjecture is true. Yea, good luck brute forcing that. The bottom line is, it is exactly because we humans don't have much brain capacity, we invented these mathematical tools that helped us reach the unfathomable: That is how we know <span class="formula">TREE(3)</span> is way bigger than Graham's number without knowing its exact value, and how we know <span class="formula">BB(n)</span> is guaranteed to be bigger than <span class="formula">TREE(n)</span> at some point. And AI are undoutedly going to be humbled in the same way. They can invent some cleverer tools, perhaps? That will be welcome.

Another hallucination humans tend to have is thinking that the past is analogous, that we can say "history shows...". Well, history may rhyme but doesn't repeat. And this time history may not even rhyme. Industrial revolution, railway boom, optical fiber boom, whichever you want to pick. Workers during industrial revolution did riot and vandalized factories. People didn't quietly give in to being replaced by machines, and often, those who rioted were working in a factory to begin with. They had learned the new skills and still rejected how the world changed. I'd say at least we still haven't seen software developers burning down data centers yet. Railways, though over-built, they did immediately help to spring up many things that previously had no supply, such as ice from the Great Lakes, which could then be sent to all over the US (and even the entire world). But today's AI revolution only seems to provide more supply to things that already have an oversupply: software, short-form videos, art... Also, if self-driving cars become widely adopted, that's going to compete in the gig economy, too. Totally not like gig work is having an oversupply of workers, right? (\*Chuckles\* I'm in danger!) Optical fiber... It's not even comparable to data centers. One has to have goldfish memory to not remember that we just had a GPU data center boom right before all these LLM stuff - because of cryptocurrency! How are they faring as "infrastructure"?

You know how OpenAI and Anthropic's employees are actively warning how AI might destroy humanity by 2030? Actually, I wouldn't be surprised if that date comes even earlier. Why? Because this estimate is based on hypothetical scenarios that are heavily influenced by past sci-fi media, that AI will start to improve itself recursively, ultimately achieving exponential growth, becoming Artificial Superintelligence (ASI), surpassing all human intelligence combined. I'd say that assumption is pretty cartoonish, and unlikely to happen soon. So why am I saying the "doomsday" might come even faster? **Because humanity probably doesn't even need ASI to destroy ourselves. And by focusing so much on the ASI narrative, we lose sight of real danger that could be brewing all along.** It's like nuclear weapons. It's easier to imagine a nuclear war scenario where nuclear powers actively declare war on each other, than a war triggered by something much more mundane, like a false alarm. Likewise, how AI is replacing entry-level jobs is something that grabs the attention of headlines, but very few are talking about how entry-level job openings were already disappearing before ChatGPT came out. It is easier to notice how South Korea has such a ridiculously low birth rate (around 0.7 while 2.1 is the replacement rate), and point to that as a model how ultra-Capitalist societies might end up being like, than to notice how Thailand has somehow also hit a 1.0 birth rate recently, while the country hasn't been able to recover from the birth rate decline even when UN predicted they should be. The global economy is already in serious troubles, and I feel as uncertain as ever about what future holds. [Technofeudalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technofeudalism), perhaps that's where we're moving towards? But even that, I think it misses some points. In a feudal society, landlords lease lands to serfs, and serfs produce food on these lands and feed both the landlord and themselves. Notice that they are at least consumers themselves, too, consumer to their product, namely, food. But in the "technofeudalist" system, "serfs" that "plough" on an online platform (such as Facebook, Youtube, etc.) need someone else to consume their content to begin with, and since your "field" doesn't exactly grow food, these consumers need to be monetizable, i.e. they need to have some other job to have money to give to you, which is... not a guarantee in today's economy, let's just say. But surely we don't need more people to farm to have enough food for everyone, either. So we end up with this weird system where we for sure have enough food for everyone, but we can't distribute it to them because they don't work which is in turn because they're replaced by AI? Yea, right. If nothing else, one thing the recent drama happened between OpenAI and the math community told us is that, perhaps we don't need to worry about an ASI that's not aligned with humanity: humans are not aligned with humans to begin with.

And perhaps there's another thing mathematicians can teach software engineers. For mathematicians, teaching and passing the knowledge to the next generation is a big part of being a mathematician (as is said in Grant's video), so they freak out when they realize that their students' career paths are being destroyed. But the software industry is more aligned with Capitalist incentives. If you're a good developer, there will always be a better developer who can crush you. If you don't use AI, you're falling behind. Managers push you, competitions push you, your work ethics push you. Better product and more efficiency is all there is. Nobody says "Hey, we haven't been hiring any junior devs for so long." and many are even exhilarated when AI could finally automate the work they do. And our bosses? Typically don't care at all. As much as we glorify those who outdo themselves and achieve great things through endeavors, what of those who come after us? What's left for them?
